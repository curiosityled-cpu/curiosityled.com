## How do you use coaching skills in a one-on-one meeting?

A coaching-style 1:1 runs on open questions and active listening, not status updates. The manager's job is to surface the team member's own judgment, then close the loop on what they will do differently. The dialogue below works for newly promoted managers and veterans alike, and pairs well with the role-specific guidance on the [talent manager page](/talent-manager).

### Example dialogue: opening a coaching-style 1:1

Skip "How's it going?" Try: "What's the one thing on your plate that's taking more energy than it should?" Then stay quiet. If the answer is vague, follow with "What would make that easier?" The opening sets the contract: this is their agenda, not yours.

### Example dialogue: probing without leading

When they describe a problem, resist supplying the fix. Ask: "What options have you already considered?" and "What would you try if I weren't in the room?" Leading questions ("Have you thought about just talking to Sarah?") teach dependence. Close with: "What's your next step, and when will you take it?"

## How does coaching differ from giving direct feedback?

Coaching pulls the answer out of the manager's report through questions; feedback puts your observation directly in front of them. Both are legitimate, and strong managers switch deliberately. Coaching fits judgment calls where the other person owns the fix. Direct feedback fits clear performance facts, safety issues, or anything a healthcare or BPO operation cannot afford to leave ambiguous.

### When feedback must be direct, not coached

If a nurse leader tolerates missed handoff documentation, or a BPO team lead lets QA scores slide, coaching questions read as avoidance. State the observation, the impact, and the expectation. Coaching comes afterward, when the person is deciding how to close the gap. HR leaders building manager capability programs should look for training that teaches both modes, not coaching as a universal default; Skillsoft's leadership development catalog is one example of a provider covering both conversation types.

### Example dialogue: difficult feedback without judgment

> "In Tuesday's 1:1 you told Priya her escalation was overreacting. She went quiet. I need escalations handled without dismissing the person. What got in the way?"

It names behavior, impact, and expectation without labeling character. Then shift to coaching: "How do you want to reopen it with her?" Managers who run this closed loop, feedback then coached follow-through, build the behavioral evidence HR needs; see Curiosity Led's manager development approach.

## How do you coach an underperforming team member without micromanaging?

Micromanagement is what happens when a manager holds the diagnosis in their own head. Coaching an underperformer means transferring the diagnosis to them: name the gap with evidence, then ask them to explain what is causing it and what they will change. That combination of clear accountability plus genuine questioning is what separates coaching from hovering.

### Example dialogue: naming the gap and asking for the diagnosis

> **Manager:** "I want to talk about the discharge-summary turnaround. Three of the last five were late, and two needed rework. That is the gap I see. What is driving it from your side?"
>
> **Team member:** "The handoffs from the night shift are messy, so I start behind."
>
> **Manager:** "What would you change first if you owned the fix?"

Notice the structure: the manager states observable facts, not character judgments ("you're careless"), then stops talking. The team member supplies the diagnosis, which means they own the fix. If the manager instead prescribed "start at 7am and check the queue," they would get compliance, not judgment changed, and the same gap reappears next month in a different form.

### The follow-up loop that prevents micromanaging

Agree on one change, a checkpoint date, and what evidence will show it worked, then let the checkpoint do the checking instead of daily pings. A single 15-minute review a week later, asking "what worked, what did not," closes the loop without hovering. This is the closed loop HR leaders should look for when auditing how managers in healthcare and BPO teams handle difficult feedback; the same structure works in career conversations and 1:1s. ## What questions should managers ask when coaching instead of advising?

Advising gives the manager's answer. A good question surfaces the employee's judgment, which is what you actually need evidence of. Gallup's manager research links manager quality to a large share of team engagement variance, so the questions below are designed to reveal how a manager thinks, not just what they say. Test your own managers against these in real 1:1s, and log what changes.

### Questions for delegation moments

- "What would you do first if I wasn't here this week?" surfaces ownership and prioritization.
- "Where do you want a check-in, and where do you want full discretion?" tests self-assessed readiness against yours.
- "What's the risk you'd flag to me before I'd spot it myself?" reveals judgment about blind spots.

### Questions for career and readiness conversations

- "What decision this quarter are you proudest of, and what would you change?" is evidence of reflection, not activity.
- "What role would stretch you without breaking the team?" surfaces bench strength and succession gaps.
- "What would need to be true for me to hand you this next?" makes readiness criteria explicit and testable.

## When should a manager coach versus when should they just direct?

### A three-question decision rule

Ask three questions before every conversation. How high are the stakes? How urgent is the decision? How experienced is the person with this exact task?

Coach when stakes are low or time allows and the person has partial experience: a new charge nurse working through how to handle a scheduling conflict, or a BPO team lead deciding how to rebalance queue coverage. Direct when stakes are high, time is short, or the person is genuinely new: a patient safety incident, a client escalation with a contractual SLA, a compliance breach. Direct first, coach after the fire is out.

### Why operations-heavy teams over-direct

Healthcare and BPO managers default to directing because their days are measured in throughput, so directing feels faster. It is faster once, then slower forever: the team stops building judgment, and every decision routes back through the manager. The fix is not coaching everything. It is naming the mode out loud: "This one I'm deciding. The next one, walk me through your call and I'll hold back."

## How do you build coaching skills if you've never been trained?

Most newly promoted managers were never coached themselves, so the skill has to be built deliberately. Start with the moments you already have: your next 1:1, your next piece of difficult feedback. Coaching is learned through repetition in the flow of work, not through a workshop you attend once.

### Practice in the flow of work

Pick one skill per week, such as asking a question before offering an answer, and use it in real conversations. Pair with a peer manager and debrief each other after difficult conversations. Psychological safety makes this practice stick: managers learn faster when they can admit what went wrong without penalty, as Amy Edmondson's research on team learning shows.

### Training options and what they cost you

Structured options include LinkedIn Learning's coaching courses, vendor programs from CoachHub or BetterUp, and internal peer-coaching circles. The cost is mostly manager time: budget one hour a week for practice and debrief, and treat missed weeks as a signal the program is failing.

## How do HR leaders know coaching skills actually changed manager behavior?

### Activity metrics vs behavioral evidence

Activity metrics tell you what happened. Session counts, course completions, assessment scores. They prove spend occurred, not that judgment changed.

Behavioral evidence shows the difference in what managers actually do: how they open a difficult feedback conversation, whether they ask before advising, whether delegation shifts from rescuing work to handing it over. That evidence comes from real moments, 1:1s and feedback conversations, captured in the flow of work.

### What a closed loop looks like

A defensible story to the board has three parts. A baseline of how newly promoted managers currently handle a specific moment, like underperformance. A development intervention targeted at that moment. Then a second read showing judgment changed, not just attendance.

The honest gap: most platforms, including Curiosity Led, depend on managers participating in the capture. If a manager opts out, you have no evidence for that person. Ask any vendor, BetterUp, CoachHub, GloCoach, or us, exactly how they handle that, and treat a vague answer as the answer.

## FAQ

### What is the LinkedIn coaching skills for leaders and managers course, and is it worth it?

It is a short LinkedIn Learning course led by instructor Sara Canaday covering fundamentals: open questions, active listening, feedback, and knowing when to coach versus direct. It works as a first exposure for newly promoted managers. Its limit is the same as any course: watching scenarios is not the same as practicing them in your own 1:1s.

### Can coaching skills be learned online, or do managers need live practice?

Online courses teach the vocabulary and example dialogue; live practice makes the skills stick. The gap shows up on Monday, when a manager who aced the course still botches a difficult feedback conversation. Pair online learning with real repetitions: one skill per week used in actual conversations, plus a peer manager to debrief with afterward.

### How long does it take for a new manager to become a competent coach?

Plan on weeks, not a workshop. A reasonable cadence is one skill per week practiced in real conversations, with about an hour weekly for practice and debrief. A manager who runs that loop consistently can handle coaching-style 1:1s and difficult feedback within a quarter. Missed weeks are the signal the program is failing, not a reason to extend the timeline.

### What's the difference between coaching and mentoring skills for managers?

Mentoring transfers the mentor's experience: advice, path guidance, and answers drawn from having done the role. Coaching pulls the other person's judgment out through questions, so they own the diagnosis and the fix. Managers need both, and the skill is switching deliberately: coach when the person can find the answer, mentor or direct when they genuinely cannot.

### How do you measure whether manager coaching is working?

Activity metrics like session counts and course completions prove spend occurred, not that judgment changed. Behavioral evidence does: how a manager opens a difficult feedback conversation, whether they ask before advising, whether delegation stops rescuing work. The defensible version is a closed loop: a baseline of a specific moment, a targeted intervention, then a second read showing the behavior changed.
